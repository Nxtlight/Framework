# Framework
A computational toolbox for analyzing the performance of complex adaptive systems. Numerous tools with examples and case studies.

The NxtLight Framework models networks in a way that captures something most other tools miss: the connections change. In the real world, people form new friendships and lose old ones; power grids add and remove transmission lines; neurons strengthen some connections and weaken others based on experience. The Reed Framework simulates this constant rewiring and then asks: given that the network is always changing, what patterns emerge? What hidden structures form? And crucially — which pieces of the network are actually in charge?

The NxtLight Framework does not apply one analytical method and call it done. It applies ten different analytical lenses simultaneously, each asking a distinct question about the same evolving network. This matters because each lens can reveal something the others miss — and when multiple lenses agree, the conclusion is far more trustworthy than when only one does.
Here is what each lens looks for, in plain terms:

Finding the Hidden Drivers

🎯  Who Is Causing What — Imagine you are watching a room full of people, and you notice that every time one particular person speaks, the conversation shifts noticeably in a new direction — even when they are not the loudest voice. This module identifies those quiet drivers. It measures which nodes in the network are genuinely causing changes in their neighbors, as opposed to simply moving together because they share a common influence from somewhere else. It is the difference between a trend-setter and someone who merely follows trends at the same time as other followers.

🏆  Who Are the True Hubs — Not all well-connected people are equally important. This module scores every node in the network on three separate measures — how many shortest paths run through it, how diverse its neighborhood is, and how strongly it drives others — and combines them into a single ranking. A node that ranks highly on all three is genuinely critical, in a way that no single measure alone can confirm.

Understanding the Health and Stability of the System

📊  Is the System Stable or Heading for Chaos — Think of a spinning top. When it is spinning fast and upright, small nudges do not bother it. As it slows, the same nudge starts to matter more and more, until eventually the top topples. This module measures the equivalent of that wobble for a network — whether small disturbances are dying out (healthy, stable system) or amplifying (a system drifting toward unpredictable behavior). It is one of the earliest warnings available that something is changing.

🔍  What Are the Governing Rules — Given a time series of measurements from a network — the kind of data you might collect from sensors on a power grid, or daily polling data from a social network — this module asks: what simple equation would explain what we are seeing? Rather than fitting a complicated black-box model, it finds the smallest, most human-readable rule that describes the behavior. "The average opinion decays toward zero unless there is a strong minority pushing back" is the kind of thing this module can discover from data alone.

🗺️  How Many Things Are Really Changing — A network might track fifty different variables, but the actual behavior of the system might only be changing along two or three meaningful dimensions — like how a weather system with thousands of temperature readings is really governed by a handful of large-scale patterns. This module strips away the noise and identifies how many genuine degrees of freedom the network actually has, and what they are. A system that looks complex often turns out to be simpler than it appears — and knowing that is scientifically valuable.

✅  Is What We Are Seeing Real — Any simulation can produce interesting-looking patterns by accident. This module is the skeptic in the room. It takes the network's output, compares it to a large collection of randomly scrambled versions of the same data, and asks: is the structure we are seeing genuinely there, or could it have appeared by chance? If the real output is dramatically different from the scrambled versions, the structure is real. This is the equivalent of a scientific control group, built directly into the analytical pipeline.

Mapping the Structure of the Network

🔗  How Well Connected Is Everything — Imagine a city whose neighborhoods are connected by bridges. If all the bridges are strong and numerous, traffic flows freely everywhere. If just one or two bridges carry almost all the traffic, losing one of them — whether from a flood, a failure, or simple overload — could cut the city in half. This module measures exactly that: how robustly connected the network is, which parts are at risk of becoming isolated, and how quickly information (or a contagion, or a failure) could spread from any one point to any other.

👥  Who Forms Groups With Whom — In any social gathering, people naturally cluster — old friends in one corner, colleagues in another, strangers near the food. Networks do the same thing. This module identifies which nodes have formed tight clusters — communities that interact more with each other than with the rest of the network — and tracks how those communities evolve. A community that is tightening rapidly may be about to become isolated from the rest. One that is dissolving may signal that the network is homogenizing, or fragmenting.

🎮  Who Would Need to Be Reached to Steer the System — If you wanted to change the direction of a complex system — calm down a contagion, stabilize a financial market, guide a neural circuit toward a healthier pattern — which specific nodes would you need to intervene on? This module identifies the minimum set of nodes that, if externally guided, would give you theoretical control over the entire network's trajectory. The answer is often counterintuitive: the most influential-seeming nodes are not always the ones you need to reach.

Reading the Shape of Time

🌀  What Is the Shape of the System's Behavior Over Time — If you traced the path of a pendulum in space, you would draw an oval — a simple, closed loop. If you traced a chaotic system, you would draw something that never quite repeats — a tangled, fractal structure. This module takes the network's history and asks: what is the shape of its trajectory through all its possible states? A clean loop means the system is cycling predictably. An open, spreading shape means it is exploring new territory. This geometric picture of behavior over time reveals things that no snapshot in time can show.

🌡️  How Complex Is the Behavior Over Time — Some systems are so regular they are predictable to the minute. Others are so erratic they are effectively random. The most interesting systems — and the most computationally powerful — live in the middle: complex enough to be flexible and adaptive, but not so chaotic that they are unreliable. This module measures precisely where on that spectrum the network sits at any given moment, and whether it is moving toward order or toward disorder.

How the Ten Lenses Work Together

The real power of the NxtLight Framework is not any single lens — it is what happens when all ten run at the same time on the same network. The framework is continuously watching a set of four key indicators simultaneously: how well connected the network is, how strong its community structure is, how hard it would be to steer from the outside, and how much information is actively flowing between its members. When these four indicators all move in the same direction at the same time, something significant is happening. When they disagree — when the network looks stable by one measure but troubled by another — that disagreement is itself the most interesting finding.

Think of it like a medical checkup. A doctor does not rely on a single vital sign. They look at blood pressure, heart rate, temperature, and a blood panel, and they pay special attention when multiple readings are all slightly off in the same direction, even when each one alone seems within normal range. The NxtLight Framework does the same thing for networks — and it does it continuously, in real time, as the simulation runs.

A concrete example:  A network might look perfectly stable by the stability measure — no sign of chaos. But at the same time, the community structure is tightening rapidly and the algebraic connectivity is quietly falling. The Nxtlight Framework would flag this combination as "polarization onset" — the network is organizing itself into increasingly isolated camps that are still connected, but barely. That early warning, invisible to any single method, is exactly what the framework is designed to provide.

Built to Be Shared — and Built to Grow

The NxtLight Framework is designed from the ground up to be open source — meaning anyone, anywhere, can download it, use it, study it, and build on it, free of charge and without asking permission. This is a deliberate choice, and an important one.

Science advances fastest when tools are shared. When a researcher in Tokyo can pick up the same framework that a researcher in Indianapolis developed, apply it to their own problem, and publish results that can be directly compared, the pace of discovery accelerates. When those results reveal that the framework is missing something — a new analytical method, a better way of measuring community structure, a domain-specific module for ecological networks — the open-source model means they can build that addition and share it back. The framework grows with the community that uses it.

Adding a new analytical capability to the Reed Framework is deliberately designed to be simple. A researcher writes a single Python file — typically a few dozen lines of code — drops it into a folder, and adds one line to a configuration file. The framework finds it automatically at the next startup, runs it alongside all the existing analyses, and includes its results in the output. No permission required, no complex integration work, no modification of the underlying system. A researcher working on disease ecology could add a module that measures species interaction diversity; one working on neuroscience could add a module that tracks synchronization patterns specific to neural circuits; one working on supply chains could add a module that measures supply redundancy. Each addition enriches the framework for everyone who uses it.

The project will be hosted publicly on GitHub — a widely used platform where software projects are shared and developed collaboratively — at an address that will be published when the first stable version is released. Researchers will be able to download the framework, report problems, suggest improvements, and contribute new modules, all through a transparent and documented process. Contributions from graduate students, independent researchers, and domain experts are explicitly welcomed alongside those from academic institutions.
In short:  The NxtLight Framework is not a finished product delivered from on high. It is a living research tool that improves every time a researcher anywhere in the world discovers something new about complex networks and builds that discovery into a plugin.

Who This Is For and Why It Matters

The NxtLight Framework is for anyone who studies systems where the connections matter as much as the things being connected. That is a description that fits an enormous range of researchers: epidemiologists trying to understand why some outbreaks explode and others fizzle; neuroscientists mapping how different brain regions drive each other's activity; ecologists studying how the removal of one species ripples through a food web; economists modeling how risk spreads from one financial institution to its counterparties; sociologists watching how communities form and polarize in online networks; engineers designing power grids that can survive the failure of their most critical nodes.

In each of these fields, the standard tools tend to be either too simple — measuring one thing at a time, missing the interactions — or too specialized, built for one domain and unable to speak to the others. The Reed Framework is designed to sit in the middle: rigorous enough to produce results that researchers trust, general enough to apply across domains, and transparent enough that a researcher can look inside and understand exactly what every number means and where it came from.

The deeper reason this work matters is that the most consequential events in modern life — financial crises, epidemic surges, cascading power failures, the rapid spread of misinformation — are all network events. They are not caused by a single bad actor or a single point of failure. They emerge from the structure and dynamics of connection. Building better tools for understanding those structures is not an academic exercise. It is preparation for a world in which the things that surprise us most are always, in retrospect, the things we were not watching closely enough — because we were looking at individual pieces rather than the web that holds them together.
